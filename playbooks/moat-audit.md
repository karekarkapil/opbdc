# Moat audit

*Companion to Chapter 18, "Moats in an Age of Abundance", of* 1 Person, Billion Dollar Conglomerate *(2026 edition). Last reviewed: September 2026.*

## What this is

A quarterly, written check of what protects each company when code is no longer a moat. The question it answers is not "what is hard to build?" but **"what is hard to generate?"** It holds the five questions, a scoring sheet for the seven moats, and a worked example.

## How to use it

1. Once a quarter, for each company, answer the five questions in writing. Allow about an hour per company.
2. An agent may draft the evidence column from your context kit, customer file and dashboard. **You set the scores.** Score harshly: a moat you cannot show evidence for scores low.
3. Put the "one to deepen" actions into next week's briefs, and the test for "the one we are pretending" on your calendar.
4. File the audit with the quarter's strategy review. Compare with last quarter's.

---

## The five questions

1. **Score the seven moats** from 0 to 5: distribution, trust, data and context, relationships, operations, speed of learning, honest switching costs.
2. **The weekend test.** If a well-funded competitor with the same agents copied everything visible about this company this weekend, what would we still have on Monday?
3. **The platform test.** Which of our features could a large platform or model maker add next quarter? What would we have left?
4. **The one to deepen.** Which single moat will we strengthen this quarter, and with what concrete actions?
5. **The one we are pretending.** Which moat do we believe we have but cannot prove? Test it.

## Scoring guide

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | We do not have this at all |
| 1 | We believe we have it, with no evidence |
| 2 | Early evidence: a few examples |
| 3 | Clear evidence, but a competitor could match it within a year |
| 4 | Strong evidence, built over time, hard to match within a year |
| 5 | Strong evidence, and it grows on its own every week |

A company that scores low on every moat is not necessarily a bad company. It may be a fine source of cash for a while. Run it as a short-lived asset, not a long-term one, and write that choice in the [decision log](../context-kit/decision-log.md).

## What is not a moat

Check that none of your scores rests on these.

- [ ] **Your code.** Valuable to you, easily reproduced by others.
- [ ] **Your prompts and agent instructions.** Useful know-how, but a competitor can arrive at similar instructions in an afternoon.
- [ ] **Your choice of model or tools.** Everyone can use the same ones.
- [ ] **Being first.** It matters only if you used the head start to build one of the seven moats.
- [ ] **Features.** A long feature list is a long list of things to be copied.
- [ ] **Generic content.** Abundant, and worth little.

## Legal moats, re-read

> This is not legal advice. Take trademark, trade secret and copyright questions to a qualified lawyer where you operate. See the [legal starter](legal-starter.md).

- [ ] **Trademarks:** name and marks registered where you trade? The most useful legal protection for a small company.
- [ ] **Trade secrets:** recipes, supplier terms, context kit and customer data identified, and actually kept confidential (check access in the [permission audit](permission-audit.md))?
- [ ] **Copyright:** a real human hand in your distinctive creative assets, recorded? Work generated entirely by machines may not qualify.
- [ ] **Patents:** rarely the right tool for a small company; almost never for software built quickly with agents.
- [ ] **Design system and voice:** not legal moats, but brand moats. Consistent across every page?

## Worked example: Copper Pot Mixers, Q3

Copper Pot Mixers is a fictional company used for illustration. It is the company from the start of Chapter 18: a competitor has just launched an identical one-tap reorder system, built in a weekend.

| # | Moat | Score | Last quarter | Evidence | Action this quarter |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Distribution you own | 3 | 3 | 140 bar managers on the email list; about two-thirds of orders are repeat orders placed without a reminder | Start a monthly "new serve" note for the list |
| 2 | Trust and reputation | 4 | 4 | Every damaged-goods report answered by the founder within a day; no unresolved complaints this year; three new bars came by referral | Keep; ask two happy bars for a real testimonial, with permission |
| 3 | Proprietary data and context | 4 | 3 | A year of each bar's ordering history; customer file with dated quotes from 60 bar visits | Use order history to suggest festival-weekend quantities |
| 4 | Relationships | 4 | 4 | Founder has visited every account; runs staff tastings; managers call her directly | Keep; visit the ten largest accounts again before the festival season |
| 5 | Operations in the physical world | 3 | 3 | Recipes and roasting process; deliveries arrive on the fixed day | Document the roasting process as a trade secret; restrict access |
| 6 | Speed of learning | 3 | 2 | Weekly review held 11 of 13 weeks; reorder page improved twice this quarter | Hold all 13 next quarter |
| 7 | Switching costs, earned honestly | 1 | 2 | She believes reorder history and suggested quantities keep bars with her, but has no evidence yet (the scoring guide's 1) | Let bars export their order history at any time; run the test below |
| | **Total** | **22 of 35** | 21 of 35 | | |

**Weekend test.** The competitor copied the screens. On Monday Copper Pot still has: the bar managers who know the founder by name, a year of each bar's ordering history, a delivery routine that arrives on the right day, recipes that taste better, a reputation for keeping promises in a small industry where everyone talks, and a weekly rhythm that has already improved the feature twice.

**Platform test.** A large ordering or payments platform could add "repeat last order" as a built-in feature next quarter. What would be left: everything in the weekend-test answer. The reorder page itself is not the moat; the history, the relationships and the deliveries behind it are.

**The one to deepen: proprietary data and context.** Actions:
1. Brief an agent to turn each bar's order history into suggested quantities before festival weekends, shown on the reorder page. Owner: founder. Spec by [date].
2. Add a question to every bar visit about upcoming events, recorded in the customer file the same day.
3. Confirm the privacy policy covers this use of order history (see the [privacy policy template](legal-templates/privacy-policy.md)).

**The one we are pretending: switching costs.** The founder believes bars would not leave because their history lives in her system. She cannot prove it, so she scores it 1 this quarter (down from last quarter's hopeful 2). Test: in the next ten bar visits, ask what it would take to switch to the competitor, and record the answers verbatim. If at least half mention something she provides that the competitor cannot, the score can rise next quarter; if not, she stops relying on it.

## Across a portfolio

Some moats can be shared across companies:

- **The founder's reputation** spans every company.
- **The machine itself:** the ability to launch, learn and improve faster, company after company.
- **Knowledge of a customer** can serve several adjacent companies.

Some cannot. **Customer data and customer lists belong to each company, and may not be pooled without consent.** A founder's personal audience is the founder's; a company's customer list is the company's. Score each company's moats separately, and note which scores depend on a shared moat. See the [portfolio map](../portfolio/portfolio-map.md).
