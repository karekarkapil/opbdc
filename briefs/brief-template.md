# Brief template

*Companion to Chapters 3 and 4, "Context Is the Company" and "Managing Agents", of* 1 Person, Billion Dollar Conglomerate *(2026 edition). Last reviewed: September 2026.*

## What this is

A **brief** is what you give an agent for one particular job. Your context kit is the standing knowledge of the company; the brief is the assignment. It is the single habit that most improves the work you get back.

## How to use it

1. Copy the template below into a new message or file.
2. Fill in all six parts. If a part feels unnecessary, write one line anyway: it is usually the part that saves you.
3. Point at files in your [context kit](../context-kit/) rather than retyping what is already written down.
4. For anything larger than a small task, add the "plan first" block at the end.

---

## The template

```
Goal:
[What you want, in one or two sentences, including why.]

Context:
[Which files to read, e.g. company brief, facts sheet, voice guide, customer file.]
[Anything specific to this job that is not written down anywhere yet.]

Constraints:
[What it must not change, spend, promise or touch.]
[Limits of size, time, money, sources, tone.]

Done:
[What finished looks like, concretely: format, length, where it is saved, what note comes with it.]

Verification:
[How the agent checks its own work before handing it back.]
[What evidence it shows you: test results, a screenshot, a sample, sources with links, a list of anything it could not confirm.]

Questions:
[When it should stop and ask you rather than guess.]
```

## Guidance for each part

| Part | Ask yourself | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Would a smart new hire know *why* this matters, not only what to do? | Stating the task without the reason, so the agent cannot handle cases you did not foresee |
| **Context** | Which files hold the facts this job needs? What do I know that is not written anywhere? | "Use our usual style" when no voice guide exists; pasting facts that already live in the facts sheet (they drift) |
| **Constraints** | What could a well-meaning agent do here that I would regret? | Leaving out the no list: the agent "improves" five things you never asked about |
| **Done** | Could I tell, in one look, whether this is finished? | "Make it better" or "a draft", with no format, length or location |
| **Verification** | What evidence would convince me, rather than an assurance? | Accepting "I checked it" instead of the list of what was checked against what |
| **Questions** | Where would I rather be asked than surprised? | No stopping points, so the agent guesses at exactly the moment it should ask |

**Evidence, not assurance.** "The tests pass" is an assurance; the test results are evidence. "I checked the prices" is an assurance; a list of each price with the line in the facts sheet it came from is evidence. Build the evidence you want into the Verification part.

## Worked example: the tasting-kit guide (from Chapter 3)

*Copper Pot Mixers is a fictional company used for illustration.* This is the kind of job you might hand over on a Monday morning.

> **Goal:** Draft a one-page guide for bar staff on making our three most popular drinks with our mixers, to include with every tasting kit. Bar managers tell us their staff don't know what to do with the bottle, so kits go unused.
>
> **Context:** Read the company brief, the facts sheet (recipes and serving sizes are there), the voice guide, and the customer file, especially the notes from bar visits in the last three months.
>
> **Constraints:** Use only recipes in the facts sheet. No claims about health or alcohol content beyond what the facts sheet says. One printed page, readable from arm's length behind a bar.
>
> **Done:** A draft in the marketing folder, with a short note on the choices you made.
>
> **Verification:** Check every quantity against the facts sheet and list any you could not find. Read it once as a bartender with thirty seconds to spare, and tell me what you would cut.
>
> **Questions:** Ask me before you include any drink that is not in the facts sheet, or if the customer notes suggest a different top three.

Notice how much of the brief points at files that already exist. The first brief you write takes twenty minutes. Once your company is written down, most briefs take two.

## The two-minute brief

Once your context kit exists, most briefs shrink to this. The files carry the detail.

```
Goal: [one sentence, with the why]
Read: [file names from the context kit]
Don't: [the one or two things most likely to go wrong]
Done when: [format and location]
Show me: [the evidence]
Ask before: [the stopping point]
```

Example:

```
Goal: Reply to this week's stock questions so bars can plan their weekend menus.
Read: facts sheet, voice guide, support playbook.
Don't: promise a delivery day that the facts sheet's schedule does not give.
Done when: replies drafted in the support queue, one per message.
Show me: for each reply, the facts-sheet line it relies on.
Ask before: anything about a damaged product, a discount or a delivery outside Bengaluru.
```

## The "plan first" add-on (for larger jobs)

For anything bigger than a small task, add this block. Reading a plan takes a minute; throwing away a day of wrong work takes a day. (Chapter 4: the delegation loop.)

```
Plan first:
Before you start, send me your plan: the steps, the files you will read and change,
what you will produce, and what could go wrong. Wait for my reply before executing.

Checkpoints:
Stop and show me your work after [the plan / the first working version / the tests pass /
the first 10 items]. Do not continue past a checkpoint without my go-ahead.

Budget:
Spend no more than [time or money] on this. If you reach it, stop and tell me where you are.
If an approach fails twice, stop and ask rather than trying it a third time.
```

**Size the work to something checkable.** "Build the ordering system for bars" is too big for one brief. "Build the page where a bar manager sees past orders, with the tests described in the spec" is about right. "Rename this button" is too small; batch it with others.
